#+TITLE: gefs+: a frugal gefs that 9front still reads
#+DATE: 2026-10-06

gefs+ is this port with a different write policy: when the disk is
written, how many =fsync= calls one commit serves, how flushes are issued.
The format stays 9front's, so Ori's gefs reads and writes a gefs+ volume
and a cleanly unmounted one is a plain gefs volume.

First written 2026-09-28 (commit 8ceb7d7) around commits on a beat and an
=fsync= that keeps order without waiting for the disk.  A review on
2026-10-06 reordered it: =fsync= stays durable, and the first work is to
make durable =fsync= cheaper (group commit) and to bound the background
commits by age, memory and space.  Items 2 and 3 below are implemented;
the rest is plan.

* Why

With APST on (the X1 Nano, kernel 131) the SSD drops to PS4 100 ms after its
last command.  A wake-up therefore costs roughly 150-170 ms of an awake drive
whatever it writes, and the number of wake-ups is what costs power.  The bytes
per wake-up (copy-on-write paths to the root, two flushes per commit) add
milliseconds; they matter for wear, hardly for the battery.  Measured on the
laptop (LAPTOP.org, nanobsd =docs/power-levers.org=): gefs wrote 1.5-2.5 times
what FFS with =log,noatime= wrote, and the drive slept 96-99 % of the time
under both.

Where gefs stood: an idle gefs writes nothing, every commit is one wake-up,
and every =fsync= was a commit (48 in 4 minutes at one =fsync= per 5 s, twice
FFS's wake-ups).

The ceiling is low.  At idle on FFS the drive is awake about 2 % of the time;
a file system that never woke it would save that 2 % of the drive's awake
power: tens of mW (estimate), about 1 % of the laptop's idle draw.  The
difference =apst-ab= measures (APST on against off), divided by about 50,
bounds it.

The FFS baseline of those runs is high: a polling loop sshed into the laptop
every 20-50 s during them (authlog, wtmpx on the FFS root), so FFS's ~7
wake-ups a minute at idle overstate it.  Re-measure with no remote logins
during the runs.

* The format is the contract

What 9front does with what gefs+ would touch, from the 9front sources in this
tree:

- Config keys (=Kconf=): 9front looks up only =retain= (=loadautos=, fs.c),
  lists every key in its conf output (=showconf=, ctl.c) and removes keys only
  by name.  Unknown keys survive its commits.
- Its check (=checkfs=, check.c) covers the free list's order and log chains,
  the deadlists, the snap tree, and every snapshot's tree and data pointers.
  It does not look for blocks that are allocated but referenced by nothing.
- The superblock (=packsb=, pack.c) is hashed over its fields only; the rest
  of the 16 KiB block is unhashed, but 9front rewrites the superblock from
  the buffer it loaded (=load.c=), so anything put there would outlive its
  commits, stale.  Not used.
- The features word (=fs->flag=) is read and printed, never checked, so it
  cannot fence off an incompatible feature: gefs+ has to stay compatible by
  construction.

Off limits: file data inline in the tree (9front expects =Kdat= to point to a
data block), another block size (=Lgblk= 14 is compiled in), compression, new
message ops or key kinds in the trees.  They would cut writes, not wake-ups,
so they would not help the battery anyway.

Items 2 and 3 change nothing on the disk: when commits happen, not what
they write.

* Priorities (2026-10-06)

1. =fsync= stays durable by default.  Order-only =fsync= is at most an
   experimental mount option (below).
2. Durable group commit: one commit serves every =fsync= it covers.
   Done.
3. Background commits bounded by dirty age, dirty memory and space, with
   explicit durability requests overriding them and a clean file system
   doing nothing.  Done; committing for dirty memory measured as a loss
   and is off by default (below).
4. Housekeeping (snapshot schedules, deferred frees) rides with commits
   that happen anyway.  Mostly so already, measured; see below, also for
   the deadlist cost found on the way.
5. An intent log only after 2-4 are measured on the laptop.  FUA for the
   superblock later still.
6. Measurement: commits, flushes and writes per workload (=vfs.gefs.*=),
   and energy per event on the laptop.

The reason for the order: 2 and 3 keep every promise an application gets
today and take wake-ups away where several things wait at once; =fsync=order=
takes them away by changing what =fsync= promises, which is a different
kind of change and needs a proof this port does not have yet.

* 1. fsync stays durable; fsync=order only as an experiment

=fsync= returns once its data is on the disk, on every mount, and so do
=O_SYNC=/=O_DSYNC= writes, =sync(2)= with =MNT_WAIT= (shutdown) and unmount.

The 2026-09-28 plan had =-o fsync=order=: =fsync= pushes the file's pages
into the tree and returns, and the next commit makes them durable; after a
crash the disk is an earlier cut of the same order, never a reordering.  If
it is ever built, it is an explicitly experimental mount option, never a
default, documented as changing the application contract (an acknowledged
=fsync= can be lost).  Before it can even claim "order is kept", these have
to be shown, by argument and by targeted crash tests, not assumed:

- UBC and dirty pages: every page an =fsync= covers is in the tree when it
  returns, including pages under write-behind or the pagedaemon at that
  moment.  genfs skips busy pages in a synchronous =putpages= and waits on
  =v_numoutput= instead; a page marked busy whose I/O is not yet issued is a
  window to rule out.
- mmap: stores through a mapping reach the tree only when the page is put;
  =msync= and =fsync= order against later =write(2)= calls has to hold.
- Truncate: a truncation and the writes after it enter the tree in call
  order, and a crash cannot show old data past the new end (the 2026-09
  truncation bugs were exactly this kind).
- Rename: rename-over without =fsync= (the "replace a file" idiom) needs the
  source's data in the tree before the rename enters it, or a crash can
  show an empty file (ext4's =auto_da_alloc=).
- Concurrent writers: two threads writing one file, or one writing while
  another fsyncs, enter the tree in an order a program could have
  observed.

No claim of SQLite or Git safety without crash tests of them (an SQLite
counter loop with =PRAGMA integrity_check= after each reset, a git commit
loop with =git fsck=), on top of the order test (=write(A); fsync(A);
write(B)=; B never ahead of A).

* 2. Durable group commit (implemented)

Each =fsync=, =O_SYNC= write and waiting =sync= needs a particular commit
to be durable, and one commit that holds several callers' changes serves
them all:

- Commits are numbered by their cut: the moment pass 0 of =fssync= takes
  the trees, under =mutlk= (=fs->cmtcut=).  =fs->cmtdone= is the last cut
  whose headers and superblocks are flushed.
- A caller first puts its pages into the tree, then asks the mutator what
  it needs: the commit after the last cut if the tree is dirty, else the
  last cut.  The question is a job on the mutator queue, so the caller's
  page writes (queued before it) are in, and no commit is running then.
- If that commit is already durable, the caller returns.  Otherwise it
  waits for =cmtdone= to reach its number.  A caller whose changes made it
  into the commit in progress waits for that one; a caller whose changes
  came after its cut waits for the next, which one request starts and
  every later caller joins.
- The requested commit runs once the jobs queued before the request are
  done, so page writes that other callers queued meanwhile go into it too.
  =vfs.gefs.groupwait= (microseconds, default 0) can hold it back longer,
  for more callers to join; when the mutator has nothing else to do that
  wait rounds up to a clock tick (10 ms at =HZ= 100).
- A failed commit, or one that cannot cover the callers (the file system
  broke and went read-only), fails every waiter with its error (EIO,
  EROFS), where =fsync= used to return 0.

Measured in the VM (=tests/fsyncgrp.c=: N threads, each writing a 4 KiB
record to its own file and fsyncing, 10 s each, one fresh 4 GB volume per
module with =retain none= (see 4 for why); a virtio disk with the host's
write-back cache, so a flush is an =fdatasync= on the host and latencies
are the host's, not an SSD's).  Before is the module built from 8ceb7d7:

| threads | before: fsyncs/s | fsyncs per commit | p50 ms | after: fsyncs/s | fsyncs per commit | p50 ms |
|---------+------------------+-------------------+--------+-----------------+-------------------+--------|
|       1 |              405 |              1.00 |    2.1 |             394 |              1.00 |    2.1 |
|       2 |              514 |              1.80 |    2.2 |             739 |              1.92 |    2.2 |
|       4 |              725 |              1.81 |    4.5 |             931 |              2.41 |    4.1 |
|       8 |              656 |              1.76 |   11.3 |            1016 |              4.79 |    6.3 |
|      16 |              665 |              1.77 |   22.4 |            1735 |              8.54 |    8.2 |

At 16 threads: 2032 commits and 4064 flushes for 17361 fsyncs, against
3766 and 7532 for 6666.  "Before" already shared commits by accident: an
fsync whose commit job found the tree clean skipped it, which caps at
about 2.  With group commit the share grows with the callers waiting:
those that arrive during a commit share the next one.

A batching window helps only under load.  With =groupwait= 2000 (in
practice a 10 ms tick when the mutator has nothing else to do): 16
threads, 15.9 fsyncs per commit, 904/s, p50 17 ms, a quarter of the
commits of no window; one thread, 78/s instead of 394, since every fsync
waits the tick.  10000: 15.9 per commit, 541/s, p50 28 ms; one thread
42/s.  The default stays 0: on a laptop fsyncs rarely come together,
and a window would tax every lone one.

Crash test (=fsyncgrp -a= streams "ack t i" to the host after each fsync
returns; QEMU is killed 5-20 s in; after the reboot every acknowledged
record must be there and the volume must check clean): 16 resets (8
threads with 4 KiB records; 16 threads with 40000-byte records, with and
without =groupwait= 2000; 4 threads with =interval= 120 and =dirtypct=
1), 60961
acknowledged records, none lost, every check clean.  QEMU's process dies
but the host's page cache does not, so this shows that the guest had
issued and completed every write and flush before =fsync= returned, not
that a disk honours its flushes.

The first series of resets lost one record (one of ten resets): a
40000-byte record across a 1 MiB boundary.  The cause was older than
group commit and worse than a crash: write-behind queued the page writes
of a window while an extending write(2) had not yet moved v_size, and a
page write that ran first dropped the data past the old size and left the
page clean.  A plain =dd bs=1m= of 300 MB of random data to the 8ceb7d7
module read back 163 MB different after a remount, and 0 after the fix
(v_size follows each chunk of the write, as in FFS; DESIGN.org).

* 3. Background commits: bounded by age, memory and space (implemented)

The mutator thread decides, between jobs:

- *Requests* (above) first.
- *Age*: a dirty tree is committed when its oldest change is
  =vfs.gefs.interval= seconds old (default 30).  The interval is a
  maximum, not a beat: anything below commits earlier.
- *Dirty memory*: optional, off by default.  =vfs.gefs.dirtypct= > 0
  commits when the blocks queued for writing pass that percent of the
  block cache.  Measured, it is not worth it: with 25 (16 MiB of the
  default 64 MiB cache) a 1.5 GB =dd= ran 145 commits and 290 flushes
  instead of 1 and 2, wrote 8 % more blocks and took 8.8-10 s instead of
  6.4; with 50, 74 commits and 7.1 s.  Memory does not need a commit: a
  full queue, or a cache out of clean blocks, is drained (written, no
  flush), and that bounds it.  The page cache is bounded by the syncer
  and write-behind, as before.  What a commit does bring back is space
  (below).
- *Space*: when the space outside the reserve falls below 128 MiB, or half
  the reserve is gone, at most once a second: what was freed since the
  last commit is free only after one.  A 4 GB volume filled by =dd= to
  ENOSPC, emptied with =rm= and filled with 2 GB again took 2 such
  commits, and checks clean.
- *Lazy updates* alone (atime, frees a commit leaves for the next one)
  after =Lazymax= (300 s), as before.
- A clean file system: the mutator sleeps with no timeout.  It only sleeps
  with one while something is dirty.

The syncer still pushes dirty pages into the tree every =kern.syncdelay=
(30 s), and the commit no longer follows at once: the pages count as
dirty since the syncer's previous pass, so with the default interval the
syncer's pass commits as before, and a longer one delays the commit.
Metadata changes enter the tree when they are made; =write(2)= data enters
it at the syncer's pass, or at once for writes bigger than the 1 MiB
write-behind window.  So a change made without =fsync= reaches the disk
within =interval= (metadata) or about =interval= + =syncdelay= (data), at
worst.  =sync(2)= asks for a commit now; shutdown and unmount wait for
one.

Counters: =vfs.gefs.commits_{sync,age,dirty,space,lazy}= (why the mutator
committed), =requests= and =joined= (durability requests, and those that
started no commit of their own), besides =commits=, =skipped=, =writes=,
=flushes=.

On the laptop =powerd='s =acadapter= script would set the interval: 30 on
AC, 120 on battery.  Not done: the laptop is not touched from here.

Measured in the VM (=tests/beat.sh=: a log line every 2 s, a file made and
removed every 10 s, no fsync, 4 minutes, then 150 s idle; fresh volume,
default retention):

| module          | active: commits | flushes | writes | idle: commits | skipped syncer calls |
|-----------------+-----------------+---------+--------+---------------+----------------------|
| 8ceb7d7         |               8 |      16 |    476 |             1 |                    4 |
| interval 30     |               8 |      16 |    476 |             1 |                    0 |
| interval 120    |               2 |       4 |    120 |             1 |                    0 |

The default keeps the old behaviour exactly; 120 s takes three quarters
of the wake-ups away for this load (each commit here is about 60 block
writes and 2 flushes).  The idle commit is the last change's own, one
interval after it.

* 4. Housekeeping with the commits that happen anyway

Mostly so already, and measured: automatic snapshots are taken only inside
commits and only after changes; the frees of pruned snapshots (a sweep
after the commit that deleted them) and atime ride with the next commit,
and alone go out only after =Lazymax= (300 s).  With =retain 2@m= (a minute
snapshot taken and one pruned in every active minute) =beat.sh= ran 8
commits in its 4 active minutes, as without pruning, and in 330 s idle the
last change's age commit and one lazy commit 300 s later, which carried
the pruned snapshots' frees and the minute snapshot of the last changes.
So pruning adds no commit while anything else commits, and one per idle
period otherwise.

Left:

- When a sweep leaves a snapshot's predecessor unreferenced it deletes
  that too, and the snap tree is dirty (9front's tree code marks it), so
  that commit comes after the interval, not after =Lazymax=.  Rare with
  schedules, whose predecessors are labelled snapshots.
- On battery =Lazymax= could be longer, or follow the interval.  It bounds
  what a crash leaks: frees not yet committed are lost with it, and
  9front's check does not look for leaked blocks.  A trade of space for
  wake-ups, to decide with the laptop's numbers.

Found while measuring, and bigger than any of this: deadlist merging.
Every commit deletes the label's previous, unlabelled generation, and
=reclaimblocks= moves that generation's deadlists for blocks born before
the newest snapshot into the new generation: two snap-tree messages per
deadlist (and a synchronous write of a tail block when the target already
exists).  There is one deadlist per birth generation, so the work per
commit is the number of distinct birth generations among the blocks
killed since the newest snapshot; a new snapshot keeps the deadlists it
has, which resets the count.  =fsyncgrp= truncating its 16 files, written
over a few thousand commits, makes thousands: with one snapshot of main,
78 became about 2300 block writes per commit and 1850 fsyncs/s became 120;
deleting the snapshot brought it back.  A big truncation or =rm= of old
data under a long-lived newest snapshot is the bad case; frequent
snapshots keep it small.  It is 9front's algorithm, and a fix changes
which deadlists exist, which 9front must still read correctly.  To look
into before a gefs root on battery; this is also why the benchmarks in 2
use =retain none=.

* 5. Intent log, after measuring 2-4

For mounts that need many durable =fsync= calls without a whole commit
each, if group commit and the policy are not enough on the laptop.

- Space: one fixed extent (say 4 MiB), allocated once through the normal
  allocator, so both implementations see it as used.  No tree refers to it.
  It is recorded in the file system's conf as =gefs+log= = "addr len".
  9front never reads, frees or checks it.
- Records: a header (magic; stamp = the hash of the superblock of the commit
  they follow; sequence number; length; payload hash), then the tree
  messages of the fsynced operations.  Small writes carry their data inline.
  Big ones point at data blocks written to newly allocated places, which
  replay marks allocated again.  =fsync= appends records and flushes once.
- Reset for free: a commit changes the superblock hash, so every record
  before it stops matching.  A full extent forces a commit.
- Replay (gefs+ only, at mount): apply records through the normal upsert
  path while magic, stamp, sequence and hash all match, then commit.

Compatibility, stated honestly: after a clean unmount the log is stale and
the disk is plain gefs.  After a crash it is not crash-equivalent: if 9front
(or the boot loader) mounts first, it ignores the log and sees the last
commit, so it loses operations gefs+ acknowledged as durable.  Once 9front
commits, the stamps no longer match and gefs+ ignores the old records too.
The interop promise is clean-unmount compatibility only.

Power: one wake-up per =fsync= (or per group), as on FFS.  It buys
latency, not battery.  The biggest piece of work (format, write path,
replay, crash tests).

FUA superblocks (=B_MEDIA_FUA= where the disk reports =DKCACHE_FUA=,
instead of the second =DIOCCACHESYNC=) come after that, and must keep the
order the second flush gives today: every new data and tree block, and the
arena headers, durable before the superblock that points at them is.
FUA on the superblock write alone does not give that; the flush before it
stays.

* 6. Measuring

Counts, per workload: =vfs.gefs.*= before and after (commits by reason,
skipped, requests, joined, writes, flushes), and the drive's own counters
(=iostat=, =nvmectl power=).  The VM can give counts and latencies only:
its disk is a file on the host, and nothing there says what a wake-up
costs.

Energy per event, on the laptop, on battery:

- E_event = ∫ (P_event - P_quiet) dt over a long run, divided by the
  number of events (commits, flushes) the counters say happened in it.
- P_quiet: the same duration with the same session and the workload
  stopped, measured just before and after, to catch drift.
- Power from the battery's discharge rate (=envstat=), read only at the
  start and the end of a run (charge in mWh, the integral), never polled
  during it: polling over ssh is itself a workload (the 2026-09-28 runs
  showed this).  Counters likewise read only before and after.
- Runs long enough for the battery's reported charge to resolve the
  difference: at least 30 minutes each, alternated (quiet, event, quiet,
  ...), several of each.
- Events at a controlled rate (e.g. =fsyncgrp= at a fixed period, or
  =beat.sh=) so that E_event × rate can be compared with the measured
  difference in mean power.

What this gives: mJ per commit with APST on, the number that says whether
group commit, a longer interval or an intent log is worth anything on the
battery.
